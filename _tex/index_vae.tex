\section{Introduction}\label{sec-intro}

The USFS Forest Inventory and Analysis (FIA) Program surveys and
analyzes the forests of the United States through a nationwide network
of permanent sample locations. These plot locations are distributed
across all lands with a spatial sampling intensity of about 1 location
per 2400 ha according to an unaligned, quasi-systematic equal
probability sample design. This design is spatially dispersed with
deviation from a regular grid to avoid alignment of sample locations
with periodic population features. Statistical inference on forest
population status and change parameters is enabled by measurements of
forest vegetation traits made at these locations by field crews.

Historically, FIA sample locations in each state were visited
periodically as time, budgets, and workforce capacity allowed. This
approach offered logistical flexibility but complicated analysis and
reporting timelines. In the early 2000's the program shifted from
periodic to annual inventory protocols calling for approximately equal
numbers of plot measurements each year on schedules intended to be fixed
and consistent within states. Under this annual inventory, each plot
location in a state is assigned to one of a set of panels, and all plot
locations assigned to a panel are designated for measurement in the same
year. The panels in a state together comprise an inventory cycle, and
inventory cycles range in length from 5 or 7 years in the eastern US to
10 years in the west. Once the final panel in an inventory cycle is
complete, remeasurements at sample locations in the first panel begin
the following year. For more information regarding the FIA sample design
see \citep[ Chapter 2]{gb1, gb2}.

In general, FIA requires that a panel be complete before the associated
tabular data are pushed to its public-facing databases, which the
program endeavors to do within 1 year of panel completion. Similarly,
FIA requires a complete inventory cycle within a state to publish
official state-scale estimates of forest parameters in fulfillment of
Congressional reporting obligations. In practice, however, adherence to
the annual design described above has proven very challenging or
impossible for the FIA Program. Instead, delayed and skipped field
measurements cause unintentional lengthening of inventory cycles and/or
survey nonresponse \citep[see, e.g.,][]{sarndal_nonresponse}, at rates
that vary regionally and over time.

The underlying causes of delayed and skipped field measurements are
varied, and can be chronic (e.g., denied access to private lands
\citep{westfall2022spatial}, logistical or administrative challenges in
remote regions \citep{affleck2026inventory}, persistent workforce and
budgetary constraints \citep{conroy_thesis}) or can result from discrete
events (e.g., the COVID19 pandemic \citep{covid}). Late measurements of
temporally dynamic variables can induce a ``time lag'' bias in point
estimates when the time component of the estimand is not adjusted
accordkingly. Further, survey nonresponse can introduce nonresponse bias
to estimates of population parameters when response propensity is
correlated with the attribute of interest. Better understanding of
patterns in late and skipped measurements \citep{conroy_thesis}, and the
investigation of new sampling and estimation techniques to ameliorate
the statistical consequences of nonresponse
\citep{westfall2022estimation, westfall2026double} are active research
topics, but beyond the scope of the present study.

Of immediate practical concern for FIA and its external partners are the
strain on field staff and delays to tabular data publication that result
from delayed field measurements. Methods have been proposed to control
the measurement delay process by disaggregating FIA's annual panel
structure into subpanels while still preserving the spatial dispersion
of the FIA sample network within field season years. Disaggregation of
panels into subpanels enables intentional alignment of workloads with
anticipated data acquisition workforce capacity in each field season
year through the application of variable annual field sampling effort
within individual states.

Though the logistical advantages of variable annual effort (VAE) are
profound (i.e., it can help ensure the long-term viability of the
inventory in the context of funding and staffing volatility), the
statistical consequences of the redistribution of partial panels within
inventory cycles, and of extended and variable inventory cycle lengths,
are unknown across states and epochs. In particular, a need exists to
understand and communicate to stakeholders how adoption of VAE protocols
may influence the statistical properties of forest parameter estimates
published by FIA in fulfillment of Congressional reporting imperatives.

Therefore, our research objectives in this study are to:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  define three VAE regimens with respect to subpanels (fast and slow
  start, dropped subpanels, and extended and shortened cycle lengths)
  intended to be representative of circumstances FIA could conceivably
  encounter in program administration
\item
  simulate the influence of those three VAE regimens on state-scale,
  inventory-cycle wide estimates of total net merchantable timber volume
  for three US states with distinct forest types, ecological attributes,
  and FIA inventory cycle lengths.
\item
  visualize and discuss simulation results in the context of
  implications for FIA program operations and the management of US
  forests.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Initial Notation}\label{initial-notation}

\subsubsection{Sample locations}\label{sample-locations}

Let

\[\mathbf{s}_i = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}\] be the
bivariate spatial coordinate vector for the \(i^{th}\) FIA sample
location in a focal US state, \(i = 1,\ldots,n\), where \(n\) is the
number of all sample locations in the focal state, and \(x_1\) and
\(x_2\) are measures of longitude and latitude respectively.

\subsubsection{Micropanels}\label{micropanels}

Let

\[\mathcal{Q} = \{q_r : \ r=1, \ldots, R\}\]

be a set of ``micropanels'' \(q_r\) that partition the \(n\) sample
locations into approximately equivalently-sized subsets, such that each
\(q_r \in \mathcal{Q}\) achieves approximate spatial dispersion across
the focal state of each of the \(\mathbf{s}_i\) it contains. The \(q_r\)
defined here are meant to correspond to collections of sample locations
referred to in the FIA Program as ``Panel 70's'', which imply
\(R = 70\), or ``Panel 140's'', which imply \(R = 140\).

In CONUS states where the FIA Program is administered by the Pacific
Northwest Research Station, \(R = 140\) in present day inventory
operations. In the rest of the CONUS states, \(R = 70\). Though this
notation is intended to be flexible enough to accommodate a range of
possible values of \(R\), in this study we don't consider PNWRS states,
and so we'll only consider \(R = 70\) for practical purposes.

\subsubsection{Macropanels}\label{macropanels}

The \(R\) micropanels can be further aggregated into the set of \(M\)
macropanels

\[\mathcal{P}_M = \{P_m : \ m = 1,\ldots,M\}\] for any value of \(M\)
such that \(M \vert R\); that is, such that \(R\) is evenly divisible by
\(M\), and so the macropanels \(P_m \in \mathcal{P}_M\) are perfectly
evenly partitioned by the micropanels \(q_r \in \mathcal{Q}\) (though
the micropanels themselves may contain slightly different numbers of
\(\mathbf{s}_i\).

The macropanels described above are intended to correspond to the
``annual panels'\,' into which the \(s_i\) in each state are partitioned
for measurement scheduling purposes. \(M\), therefore, is intended to
correspond to conceivable state-level inventory cycle lengths prescribed
in the FIA program for \(R = 70\), and so in this study
\(M \in \{5, 7, 10, 14\}\). Because the micropanels are initially evenly
distributed across the macropanels, the number of micropanels per
macropanel can be denoted by \(k\) as

\[k = \frac{R}{M}\]

A general expression to recover the identities of the micropanels
contained in any macropanel \(P_m\) is given by

\[P_m = \{ q_r \in \mathcal{Q} : (m - 1) k \ + \ 1 \leq r \leq m k \}\].

\subsubsection{Macropanel Lookup
Function}\label{macropanel-lookup-function}

Conversely, to determine the identity of the macropanel to which
micropanel \(r\) belongs, we can define a macropanel lookup function

\[f(r) = \left\lceil \frac{r}{k} \right\rceil = \left\lceil \frac{r \cdot M}{R} \right\rceil\]
where the ceiling function \(\lceil x \rceil\) returns the smallest
integer not less than \(x\), and \(f(r) = m\) outputs the index of the
macropanel (\(1 \leq m \leq M\)) to which the \(r^{th}\) micropanel is
assigned.

\subsection{VAE Scenarios}\label{vae-scenarios}

The basic premise of VAE is to enable the modification of remeasurement
schedules by adjusting the macropanel assignments of some micropanels in
a focal state. However, in the scenarios explored below, all
\(\mathbf{s}_i\) retain their original micropanel assignments. We now
extend the initial notation to describe in general terms three VAE
scenarios achieved by either i) reassigning micropanels to different
macropanels, or ii) dropping fixed numbers of micropanels per macropanel
over the course of an inventory cycle.

\subsubsection{Slow Start and Fast Start
Scenarios}\label{slow-start-and-fast-start-scenarios}

\paragraph{Slow Start}\label{slow-start}

In the context of FIA, the ``slow start'' scenarios we simulated are
intended to represent a budgetary or workforce shortfall in the first
\(z\) years early in an inventory cycle, necessitating a planned
reduction of field visitation by a fixed proportion of micropanels per
macropanel in the first \(z\) macropanels in \(\mathcal{P}_M\).

Then, a budgetary or workforce recovery occurs, enabling a planned
increase of field visitation by the same fixed proportion of micropanels
per macropanel in the subsequent \(z\) macropanels. Finally, work
capacity stabilizes, and the \(k\) micropanels in the final macropanel
\(P_M\) in \(\mathcal{P}_M\) retain their original assignments (i.e.,
we're ``back on time'').

\paragraph{Fast Start}\label{fast-start}

Under the ``fast start'' scenarios we simulated, our VAE intentions are
analogous to the slow start scenario described above, but the timing of
the availability of resources of field work is reversed. That is, extra
resources for field work enable a fixed proportion of ``early''
micropanels per macropanel for the first \(z\) macropanels in
\(\mathcal{P}_M\). Annual sampling effort is then reduced by the same
proportion micropanels per macropanel for the subsequent \(z\)
macropanels to\\
``get back on schedule''; and the nominally prescribed rate of \(k\)
micropanels per macropanel is reestablished for macropanel \(M\). A more
detailed demonstrative example can be found in
Section~\ref{sec-supp-mat}.

\paragraph{Generalizing Shifted Macropanel
Boundaries}\label{generalizing-shifted-macropanel-boundaries}

If we preserve \(R\) and \(M\), but modify the original macropanel
assignments of some micropanels for VAE purposes as the slow and fast
start scenarios are intended to represent, the micropanels no longer
evenly partition the macropanels throughout \(\mathcal{P}_M\).
Therefore, it is convenient to extend our notation to represent an
arbitraty, newly constituted cycle \(\mathcal{P}^\prime_M\) following
repositioning of micropanels (i.e., to represent and facilitate the
application of a particular slow or fast start VAE regimen across any
value of \(M \in \{5, 7, 10, 14\}\)).

Let \(\mathbf{U}_M\) be the baseline uniform boundary sequence vector of
length \(M+1\), where the elements are determined by the constant
step-size \(k = \frac{R}{M}\):

\[\mathbf{U}_M = (u_0, u_1, \dots, u_M), \quad \text{where } u_m = m \cdot k\]

To then specify a slow or fast start regimen that can be applied
arbitrarily for any value of \(M\), we further define the boundary
deviation vector \(\boldsymbol{\delta}_M\) of length \(M+1\) as:

\[\boldsymbol{\delta}_M = (\delta_0, \delta_1, \ldots, \delta_M)\]

The shifted boundary sequence vector \(\mathbf{B}\) can then be obtained
by summing the baseline boundary vector and the boundary deviation
vector:

\[\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{U}_M + \boldsymbol{\delta}_M = (u_0 + \delta_0, u_1 + \delta_1, \dots, u_M + \delta_M)\]

If the originally prescribed boundaries are unshifted,
\(\boldsymbol{\delta}_M\) is an \(M + 1\) vector of \(0\)'s, and
\(\mathbf{B}\) is therefore simply the baseline boundary vector
\(\mathbf{U}_M\) under the originally prescribed distribution of
micropanels across macropanels.

\(\mathbf{B}\) then takes the form

\[\mathbf{B} = (b_0, b_1, \ldots,b_M),\] where the \(M+1\) elements
therein represent an intitial index \(b_0\) and the \(M\) new upper
micropanel boundaries; i.e., the values of \(r\) associated with the
last of each\\
of the sequence of shifted micropanels in the newly reconstituted
macropanels. Each modified macropanel \(P_m*\) (\(m* = 1,\ldots, M\))
can then be constructed by obtaining indices for micropanels from
between adjacent elements of \(\mathbf{B}\):

\[P_m* = \{ q_r \in \mathcal{Q} : b_{m-1} + 1 \leq r \leq b_m \}.\]

\paragraph{Modified Macropanel Lookup
Function}\label{modified-macropanel-lookup-function}

Because the distributions of micropanels across macropanels are no
longer uniform under these shifted configurations, the scalar ceiling
function cannot be used to determine macropanel assignment \(m\) of any
macropanel \(r\).\\
However, such a function is still needed to describe and implement the
slow start and fast start VAE scenarios.

We can define a modified macropanel lookup function \(f_b(r)\) that
counts how many upper bounds \(b_m\) from the sequence \(\mathbf{B}\)
are strictly less than \(r\), and then offsets the result by 1. Using
bracket notation (where the brackets evaluate to \(1\) if the enclosed
condition is true, and \(0\) if false):

\[f_b(r) = 1 + \sum_{m=1}^{M-1} [r > b_m]\]

Demonstrative examples of the construction of \(\mathbf{U}_M\),
\(\boldsymbol{\delta}_M\), \(\mathbf{B}\), and the use of \(f_b(r)\) can
be found in Section~\ref{sec-supp-mat}.

\subsubsection{Extended and Shortened Cycle
Lengths}\label{extended-and-shortened-cycle-lengths}

Under the slow and fast start VAE scenarios described above, the number
of macropanels in the cycle \(\mathcal{P}_M\) (i.e., \(M\), which we can
think of as corresponding to ``inventory cycle length'' in the context
of FIA) is preserved by shifting an equivalent number of micropanels
forward (for delayed field visitation) in some macropanels as are held
backward (for early field visitation) in others.

Another way VAE might be applied, for particular values of \(R\) and
\(M\), is to evenly redistribute micropanels into a modified total
number of macropanels, say
\(M^{\prime} \in \{5,7,10,14\}, \quad M^{\prime} \neq M\), where
\(M^{\prime}\) is still a factor of \(R\). If \(M^{\prime} > M\) the
cycle length is extended, and the macropanel sampling intensity \(k\)
(i.e., the annual effort) is reduced. Conversely, if \(M^{\prime} < M\)
the cycle length is shortened and the annual effort in each newly
configured macropanel \(P^\prime_m\) in in the modified cycle
\(\mathcal{P}^\prime_M\) is increased.

\paragraph{A modified macropanel lookup function for extended and
shortened cycle
lengths}\label{a-modified-macropanel-lookup-function-for-extended-and-shortened-cycle-lengths}

To map any micropanel from its originally prescribed macropanel \(P_m\)
to its new macropanel \(P^\prime_m\) under a shortened or extended cycle
length, we define a function that uses both the original macropanel
index \(m\) and the local micropanel offset \(l\) within that
macropanel.

Let:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(M, k\) be the parameters of the original configuration (e.g.,
  \(M=5, k=14\)).
\item
  \(M^\prime, k^\prime\) be the parameters of the new configuration
  (e.g., \(M^\prime=7, k^\prime=10\)).
\item
  \(m\) be the original macropanel index, where
  \(m \in \{1, 2, \dots, M\}\).
\item
  \(l\) be the local index of the micropanel within \(P_m\), where
  \(l \in \{1, 2, \dots, k\}\).
\end{itemize}

A micropanel's local macropanel coordinate (i.e., the two unique values
that determine its chronological position within its originally assigned
macropanel and cycle) \((m,l)\) maps to its static global index \(r\)
via:

\[r = (m - 1)k + l\]

In the new cycle configuration \(\mathcal{P}^\prime_M\), each macropanel
\(P^\prime_m\) contains exactly \(k^\prime\) micropanels. To determine
which macropanel (i.e., which sequence of \(k^\prime\) micropanels) the
index \(r\) falls within, we divide \(r\) by \(k^\prime\) and apply the
ceiling function \(\lceil \cdot \rceil\) to round up to the nearest
integer:

\[m^\prime = \left\lceil \frac{r}{k^\prime} \right\rceil\]

Combining both steps yields the definitive closed-form mapping function
\(\mathbf{f}(m,l)\):

\[f^\prime(m,l) = \left\lceil \frac{(m - 1) k + l}{k^\prime} \right\rceil\]

Where \(\mathbf{f}(m,l) = m^\prime\) represents the index of the
modified macropanel (\(m^\prime \in \{1, 2, \dots, M^\prime\}\)) to
which micropanel \(r\) is newly assigned and \(\lceil \cdot \rceil\)
represents the ceiling function described above.

\subsubsection{Fast Start and Slow
Start}\label{fast-start-and-slow-start}

\paragraph{Slow Start}\label{slow-start-1}

Consider a case where \(R = 70\) and \(M = 5\), which implies that
\(k = \frac{R}{M} = 14\) micropanels per macropanel. Consider further
that for \(m = \{1,2\}\), we shift the last four micropanels
\(q_r \in P_m\) forward one macropanel. In the context of FIA, this
might represent a budgetary or workforce shortfall in the first two
years in an inventory cycle, necessitating a planned reduction of field
visitation by \(\frac{2}{7}\) micropanels in the first two macropanels.

Envision then that the for \(m = \{3,4\}\), we shift the first four
micropanels \(q_r \in P_m\) backward one macropanel. This might
represent a budgetary or workforce recovery in the following two years
in the inventory cycle, enabling a planned increase of field visitation
by \(\frac{2}{7}\) micropanels in those next two macropanels. Finally,
assume that for the final macropanel, \(m = 5\) work capacity
stabilizes, and the \(k = 14\) micropanels in \(P_5\) retain their
original assignments.

Under this ``slow start'' VAE regimen, the boundaries \(b_m\) in
\(\mathbf{B}\) are explicitly assigned to be:

\[\mathbf{B} = (0, 10, 20, 38, 56, 70)\]

yielding the new macropanel assignments

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(P_1* = \{ q_r \in \mathcal{Q} \mid 1 \leq r \leq 10 \} = \{q_1,\ldots,q_{10}\}\)
\item
  \(P_2* = \{ q_r \in \mathcal{Q} \mid 11 \leq r \leq 20 \}\)
\item
  \(P_3* = \{ q_r \in \mathcal{Q} \mid 21 \leq r \leq 38 \}\)
\item
  \(P_4* = \{ q_r \in \mathcal{Q} \mid 39 \leq r \leq 56 \}\)
\item
  \(P_5* = \{ q_r \in \mathcal{Q} \mid 57 \leq r \leq 70 \}.\)
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Fast Start}\label{fast-start-1}

Alternatively, consider a case where \(R = 70\) and \(M = 7\), which
implies \(k = \frac{R}{M} = 10\) micropanels per macropanel, and that
our VAE intentions are analogous to the slow start scenario described
above, but the timing of the availability of resources of field work is
reversed. That is, extra resources for field work enable two ``early''
micropanels per macropanel for \(m = \{1,2,3\}\); annual sampling effort
is then reduced by two micropanels per macropanel for \(m = \{4,5,6\}\)
to\\
get back on schedule; and the nominal \(k = 10\) is finally
reestablished for \(m = 7\).

Under this ``fast start'' VAE regimen, the boundaries \(b_m\) in
\(\mathbf{B}\) are explicitly assigned to be:

\[\mathbf{B} = (0, 12, 24, 36, 44, 52, 60, 70)\]

yielding the new macropanel assignments

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(P_1* = \{ q_r \in \mathcal{Q} \mid 1 \leq r \leq 12 \} = \{q_1,\ldots,q_{12}\}\)
\item
  \(P_2* = \{ q_r \in \mathcal{Q} \mid 13 \leq r \leq 24 \}\)
\item
  \(P_3* = \{ q_r \in \mathcal{Q} \mid 25 \leq r \leq 36 \}\)
\item
  \(P_4* = \{ q_r \in \mathcal{Q} \mid 37 \leq r \leq 44 \}\)
\item
  \(P_5* = \{ q_r \in \mathcal{Q} \mid 45 \leq r \leq 52 \}\)
\item
  \(P_6* = \{ q_r \in \mathcal{Q} \mid 53 \leq r \leq 60 \}\)
\item
  \(P_7* = \{ q_r \in \mathcal{Q} \mid 61 \leq r \leq 70 \}.\)
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Illustration of the baseline and shifted boundary deviation
vectors}\label{illustration-of-the-baseline-and-shifted-boundary-deviation-vectors}

Continuing with the example above, the transformation from the uniform
\(R = 70\), \(M = 5\), and \(k = 14\) boundary vector to the specific
VAE regimen described can be obtained via:

\[\mathbf{U}_5 = (0, 14, 28, 42, 56, 70)\]
\[\boldsymbol{\delta}_5 = (0, -4, -8, -4, 0, 0)\]

Evaluating the vector addition:

\[\mathbf{B}_5 = (0 + 0, 14 - 4, 28 - 8, 42 - 4, 56 + 0, 70 + 0) = (0, 10, 20, 38, 56, 70)\]

\paragraph{Conditions to ensure validity of the boundary deviation
vector}\label{conditions-to-ensure-validity-of-the-boundary-deviation-vector}

To ensure that a boundary deviation vector yields a physically valid
modified boundary sequence under proposed slow start and fast start VAE
regimens (i.e., that it precludes overlapping or empty macropanels and
that it covers all \(R\) micropanels), the elements of
\(\boldsymbol{\delta}_M\) must satisfy the following three criteria.
Firstly, the outer micropanel boundaries cannot shift; that is, the
sequence \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) must always start at 0 and end at R
such that \(\delta_0 = 0\) and \(\quad \delta_M = 0\). Secondly, every
deviation must be an integer to preserve whole micropanel blocks, i.e.,
\(\delta_m \in \mathbb{Z} \quad \forall i \in \{0, 1, \dots, M\}\).
Finally, to prevent empty macro panels or inverted boundaries, adjacent
elements must maintain ordering such that
\((u_m + \delta_m) > (u_{m-1} + \delta_{m-1}), \quad \forall m \in \{1, 2, \dots, M\}\).

\paragraph{Example evaluation of the modified macropanel lookup function
for fast/slow
start}\label{example-evaluation-of-the-modified-macropanel-lookup-function-for-fastslow-start}

To locate micropanel \(q_{25}\) using the upper bounds contained in
\(\mathbf{B}\) for the \(M = 5\) slow start example described above
(i.e., \(b_1=10, b_2=20, b_3=38, b_4=56, b_5 = 70)\):

\[f_b(25) = 1 + [25 > 10] + [25 > 20] + [25 > 38] + [25 > 56]\]
\[f_b(25) = 1 + 1 + 1 + 0 + 0 = 3.\]

This correctly links micropanel \(q_{25}\) to modified macropanel
\(P_3*\).

\paragraph{Example evaluation of the modified macropanel lookup function
for extended/shortened cycle
lengths}\label{example-evaluation-of-the-modified-macropanel-lookup-function-for-extendedshortened-cycle-lengths}

\subparagraph{\texorpdfstring{Example cases for cycle length extension
(\(M=5, k=14 \implies M^\prime=7, k^\prime=10\))}{Example cases for cycle length extension (M=5, k=14 \textbackslash implies M\^{}\textbackslash prime=7, k\^{}\textbackslash prime=10)}}\label{example-cases-for-cycle-length-extension-m5-k14-implies-mprime7-kprime10}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Boundary Case A: The absolute first micropanel}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Input: \(m = 1\), \(l = 1\)
  \item
    Calculation:
    \[\mathbf{f}(1, 1) = \left\lceil \frac{(1 - 1)14 + 1}{10} \right\rceil = \left\lceil \frac{1}{10} \right\rceil = 1\]
  \item
    \emph{Result:} Destination is \textbf{\(P'_1\)} (Correct).
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Boundary Case B: An overlapping boundary micropanel}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Input: \(m = 1\), \(l = 12\)
  \item
    Calculation:
    \[\mathbf{f}(1, 12) = \left\lceil \frac{(1 - 1)14 + 12}{10} \right\rceil 
    = \left\lceil \frac{12}{10} \right\rceil = \lceil 1.2 \rceil = 2\]
  \item
    \emph{Result:} Destination is \textbf{\(P'_2\)} (Correct, as global
    micropanel \(q_{12}\) shifts into the second macro panel).
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Boundary Case C: The absolute final micropanel}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Input: \(m = 5\), \(l = 14\)
  \item
    Calculation:
    \[\mathbf{f}(5, 14) = \left\lceil \frac{(5 - 1)14 + 14}{10} \right\rceil = \left\lceil \frac{56 + 14}{10} \right\rceil = \left\lceil \frac{70}{10} \right\rceil = 7\]
  \item
    \emph{Result:} Destination is \textbf{\(P'_7\)} (Correct).
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
