\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

The USFS Forest Inventory and Analysis (FIA) Program surveys and
analyzes the forests of the United States through a nationwide network
of permanent sample locations. These plot locations are distributed
across all lands with a spatial sampling intensity of about 1 location
per 2400 ha according to an unaligned, quasi-systematic equal
probability sample design. This design is spatially dispersed with
deviation from a regular grid to avoid alignment of sample locations
with periodic population features. Statistical inference on forest
population status and change parameters is enabled by measurements of
forest vegetation traits made at these locations by field crews.

Historically, FIA sample locations in each state were visited
periodically as time, budgets, and workforce capacity allowed. This
approach offered logistical flexibility but complicated analysis and
reporting timelines. In the early 2000's the program shifted from
periodic to annual inventory protocols calling for approximately equal
numbers of plot measurements each year on schedules intended to be fixed
and consistent within states. Under this annual inventory, each plot
location in a state is assigned to one of a set of panels, and all plot
locations assigned to a panel are designated for measurement in the same
year. The panels in a state together comprise an inventory cycle, and
inventory cycles range in length from 5 or 7 years in the eastern US to
10 years in the west. Once the final panel in an inventory cycle is
complete, remeasurements at sample locations in the first panel begin
the following year. For more information regarding the FIA sample design
see \citep[ Chapter 2]{gb1, gb2}.

In general, FIA requires that a panel be complete before the associated
tabular data are pushed to its public-facing databases, which the
program endeavors to do within 1 year of panel completion. Similarly,
FIA requires a complete inventory cycle within a state to publish
official state-scale estimates of forest parameters in fulfillment of
Congressional reporting obligations. In practice, however, adherence to
the annual design described above has proven very challenging or
impossible for the FIA Program. Instead, delayed and skipped field
measurements cause unintentional lengthening of inventory cycles and/or
survey nonresponse \citep[see, e.g.,][]{sarndal_nonresponse}, at rates
that vary regionally and over time.

The underlying causes of delayed and skipped field measurements are
varied, and can be chronic (e.g., denied access to private lands
\citep{westfall2022spatial}, logistical or administrative challenges in
remote regions \citep{affleck2026inventory}, persistent workforce and
budgetary constraints \citep{conroy_thesis}) or can result from discrete
events (e.g., the COVID19 pandemic \citep{covid}). Late measurements of
temporally dynamic variables can induce a ``time lag'' bias in point
estimates when the time component of the estimand is not adjusted
accordkingly. Further, survey nonresponse can introduce nonresponse bias
to estimates of population parameters when response propensity is
correlated with the attribute of interest. Better understanding of
patterns in late and skipped measurements \citep{conroy_thesis}, and the
investigation of new sampling and estimation techniques to ameliorate
the statistical consequences of nonresponse
\citep{westfall2022estimation, westfall2026double} are active research
topics, but beyond the scope of the present study.

Of immediate practical concern for FIA and its external partners are the
strain on field staff and delays to tabular data publication that result
from delayed field measurements. Methods have been proposed to control
the measurement delay process by disaggregating FIA's annual panel
structure into subpanels while still preserving the spatial dispersion
of the FIA sample network within field season years. Disaggregation of
panels into subpanels enables intentional alignment of workloads with
anticipated data acquisition workforce capacity in each field season
year through the application of variable annual field sampling effort
within individual states.

Though the logistical advantages of variable annual effort (VAE) are
profound (i.e., it can help ensure the long-term viability of the
inventory in the context of funding and staffing volatility), the
statistical consequences of the redistribution of partial panels within
inventory cycles, and of extended and variable inventory cycle lengths,
are unknown across states and epochs. In particular, a need exists to
understand and communicate to stakeholders how adoption of VAE protocols
may influence the statistical properties of forest parameter estimates
published by FIA in fulfillment of Congressional reporting imperatives.

Therefore, our research objectives in this study are to:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  define three VAE regimens with respect to subpanels (fast and slow
  start, dropped subpanels, and extended and shortened cycle lengths)
  intended to be representative of circumstances FIA could conceivably
  encounter in program administration
\item
  simulate the influence of those three VAE regimens on state-scale,
  inventory-cycle wide estimates of total net merchantable timber volume
  for three US states with distinct forest types, ecological attributes,
  and FIA inventory cycle lengths.
\item
  visualize and discuss simulation results in the context of
  implications for FIA program operations and the management of US
  forests.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Initial Notation}\label{initial-notation}

\subsubsection{Sample locations}\label{sample-locations}

Let

\[\mathbf{s}_i = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}\] be the
bivariate spatial coordinate vector for the \(i^{th}\) FIA sample
location in a focal US state, \(i = 1,\ldots,n\), where \(n\) is the
number of all sample locations in the focal state, and \(x_1\) and
\(x_2\) are measures of longitude and latitude respectively.

\subsubsection{Micropanels}\label{micropanels}

Let

\[\mathcal{Q} = \{q_r : \ r=1, \ldots, R\}\]

be a set of ``micropanels'' \(q_r\) that partition the \(n\) sample
locations into approximately equivalently-sized subsets, such that each
\(q_r \in \mathcal{Q}\) achieves approximate spatial dispersion across
the focal state of each of the \(s_i\) it contains. The \(q_r\) defined
here are meant to correspond to collections of sample locations referred
to in the FIA Program as ``Panel 70's'', which imply \(R = 70\), or
``Panel 140's'', which imply \(R = 140\).

In CONUS states where the FIA Program is administered by the Pacific
Northwest Research Station, \(R = 140\) in present day inventory
operations. In the rest of the CONUS states, \(R = 70\). Though this
notation is intended to be flexible enough to accommodate a range of
possible values of \(R\), in this study we don't consider PNWRS states,
and so we'll only consider \(R = 70\) for practical purposes.

\subsubsection{Macropanels}\label{macropanels}

The \(R\) micropanels can be further aggregated into the set of \(M\)
macropanels

\[\mathcal{P} = \{P_m : \ m = 1,\ldots,M\}\] for any value of \(M\) such
that \(M \vert R\). That is, \(R\) is evenly divisible by \(M\), and so
the macropanels \(P_m \in \mathcal{P}\) are perfectly evenly partitioned
by the micropanels \(q_r \in \mathcal{Q}\).

In the context of FIA, each \(P_m \in \mathcal{P}\) can be thought of as
an ``annual panel'', meaning that each \(s_i \subset q_r \subset P_m\)
is scheduled to be visited by a field crew in year in the \(t^{th}\)
year in a chronological series of years indexed in the set

\[\mathcal{T} = \{t: t = 1,\ldots,T\}\] where \(T = M\). Therefore,
\(\mathcal{P}\) can be thought of as in inventory cycle, of cycle length
\(M\), composed of the annual panels \(P_m \in \mathcal{P}\), which
themselves are evenly subdivided into the micropanels
\(q_r \in \mathcal{Q}\).

\(M = T\) here is intended to correspond to the common state-level
inventory cycle lengths prescribed in the FIA program, and so in this
study \(M \in \{5, 7, 10, 14\}\). Because the micropanels are initially
evenly distributed across the annual panels, the number of micropanels
per macropanel can be denoted by \(k\) as

\[k = \frac{R}{M}\]

A general expression to recover the identities of the micropanels
contained in any macropanel \(P_m\) is given by

\[P_m = \{ q_r \in \mathcal{Q} : (m - 1) k \ + \ 1 \leq r \leq m k \}\]

\subsubsection{Macropanel Lookup
Function}\label{macropanel-lookup-function}

Conversely, to determine the identity of the macropanel to which
micropanel \(r\) belongs, we can define a macropanel lookup function

\[f(r) = \left\lceil \frac{r}{k} \right\rceil = \left\lceil \frac{r \cdot M}{R} \right\rceil\]
where the ceiling function \(\lceil x \rceil\) returns the smallest
integer not less than \(x\), and \(f(r) = m\) outputs the macropanel
index (\(1 \leq m \leq M\)).

\subsection{VAE Scenarios}\label{vae-scenarios}

We will now extend this notation to describe in general terms three VAE
scenarios achieved by either i) reassigning micropanels to different
macropanels, or ii) dropping fixed numbers of micropanels per macropanel
over the course of an inventory cycle.

\subsubsection{Slow/Fast Start}\label{slowfast-start}

\paragraph{Modified Partition Notation (Shifted
Configurations)}\label{modified-partition-notation-shifted-configurations}

For an uneven or shifted layout where subpanels are structurally
rearranged, the uniform mapping breaks down. To formalize this, we
introduce an explicit boundary sequence vector \(\mathbf{B}\) containing
\(M+1\) elements:

\[\mathbf{B} = (b_0, b_1, \dots, b_M)\]

For the specific modified configuration where \(M = 5\), the boundaries
are explicitly assigned as:

\[\mathbf{B} = (0, 10, 20, 38, 56, 70)\]

Each individual macro panel \(P_i\) (for \(i = 1, 2, \dots, M\)) is then
constructed by pulling indices between adjacent elements of the boundary
sequence:

\[P_i = \{ q_j \in \mathcal{Q} \mid b_{i-1} + 1 \leq j \leq b_i \}\]

\paragraph{Explicit Macro Panel
Contents}\label{explicit-macro-panel-contents}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(P_1 = \{ q_j \in \mathcal{Q} \mid 1 \leq j \leq 10 \}\)
\item
  \(P_2 = \{ q_j \in \mathcal{Q} \mid 11 \leq j \leq 20 \}\)
\item
  \(P_3 = \{ q_j \in \mathcal{Q} \mid 21 \leq j \leq 38 \}\)
\item
  \(P_4 = \{ q_j \in \mathcal{Q} \mid 39 \leq j \leq 56 \}\)
\item
  \(P_5 = \{ q_j \in \mathcal{Q} \mid 57 \leq j \leq 70 \}\)
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Modified Panel Lookup Function
Alternatives}\label{modified-panel-lookup-function-alternatives}

Because the subpanel distributions are no longer uniform, the scalar
ceiling function cannot be used. Below are two equivalent mathematical
approaches to express the lookup function \(f(j)\) for the shifted
configuration.

\paragraph{Approach 1: The Summation / Indicator Function
Approach}\label{approach-1-the-summation-indicator-function-approach}

We can define the lookup function \(f(j)\) by counting how many
boundaries the subpanel index \(j\) has successfully crossed. Using the
Iverson bracket notation (where the brackets evaluate to \(1\) if the
enclosed condition is true, and \(0\) if false), the macro panel index
can be calculated directly by checking membership across all intervals:

\[f(j) = \sum_{i=1}^{M} i \cdot [b_{i-1} + 1 \leq j \leq b_i]\]

Alternatively, an even more concise formulation counts how many upper
bounds \(b_i\) from our sequence
\(\mathbf{B} = (0, 10, 20, 38, 56, 70)\) are strictly less than \(j\),
then offsets the result by 1:

\[f(j) = 1 + \sum_{i=1}^{M-1} [j > b_i]\]

\paragraph{Example Evaluation}\label{example-evaluation}

To locate subpanel \(q_{25}\) using the upper bounds
\(b_1=10, b_2=20, b_3=38, b_4=56\):

\[f(25) = 1 + [25 > 10] + [25 > 20] + [25 > 38] + [25 > 56]\]
\[f(25) = 1 + 1 + 1 + 0 + 0 = 3\]

This correctly maps subpanel \(q_{25}\) to Macro Panel \(P_3\).
